A Cautionary Tale in Four-Fifths

Prime Density, Polygonal Numbers, and What Finite Data Cannot Say About Infinite Structures
Lee McCulloch-James · 2021 / 2026
Abstract. The number of primes lying between consecutive terms of polygonal number sequences appears, over finite ranges, to follow a clean power law with exponent $\frac{4}{5}$. The fit is startlingly good. It invites a geometric explanation involving hypersphere surface-area-to-volume ratios. This paper walks through the empirical discovery, lets you feel the seduction of the pattern, and then shows in a short analytic argument that the $\frac{4}{5}$ is the prime number theorem wearing a finite-range disguise. The exponent drifts inexorably toward 1 as the range extends. The pedagogical goal is twofold: to teach the analytic techniques (logarithmic differentiation, asymptotic expansion, effective exponents) that unmask such finite-range artefacts, and to illustrate the fundamental tension between empirical pattern-finding and the demands of mathematical proof. Extensions to higher-dimensional figurate sequences are treated explicitly.

1. Polygonal Numbers and Their Gaps

1.1 The unified generator

The figurate numbers of the ancient Greeks are generated by arranging pebbles into regular polygons. Triangular numbers (1, 3, 6, 10, 15, ...), square numbers (1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ...), pentagonal numbers (1, 5, 12, 22, 35, ...), and onward, are all special cases of a single quadratic formula.

Definition 1.1 — Polygonal number The $r$-th $n$-gonal number, for $n \geq 3$ and $r \geq 1$, is $$P_n(r) = \frac{(n-2)\,r^2 - (n-4)\,r}{2}.$$
Exercise 1.1 Verify that this gives $P_3(r) = \frac{r(r+1)}{2}$ (triangular), $P_4(r) = r^2$ (square), and $P_6(r) = r(2r-1)$ (hexagonal). Show that $P_6(r) = P_3(2r-1)$: every hexagonal number is triangular.

1.2 The gap between consecutive terms

The gap between consecutive $n$-gonal numbers is linear in $r$:

(1) $$P_n(r+1) - P_n(r) = (n-2)\,r + 1.$$

This is the key structural fact. The intervals widen at a steady rate. As they widen, they contain more primes. The question: how does the prime count grow as a function of $r$?

2. The Empirical Discovery

2.1 Counting primes in the gaps

For each $n$-gonal sequence and each term index $r$, define

(2) $$c_n(r) = \pi\!\bigl(P_n(r+1)\bigr) - \pi\!\bigl(P_n(r)\bigr),$$

where $\pi(x)$ is the prime counting function. Computing $c_n(r)$ for $r = 1, \ldots, 500$ and $n = 3, \ldots, 15$ is a straightforward sieving exercise. The results, on a log-log scale, are striking.

2.2 The four-fifths law

Empirical Growth Law $$c_n(r) \approx \frac{n-1}{5}\,r^{4/5}.$$

Two features stand out. First, the exponent $\frac{4}{5}$ is invariant across polygon degree $n$. Second, the coefficient $\frac{n-1}{5}$ scales linearly in $n$, with denominator 5.

The fit outperforms the PNT estimate at every point. Between the 278th and 279th square numbers (77,284 and 77,841) there are 56 primes. The empirical law predicts 55.4. The PNT predicts 49.7.

The pattern holds even under Fermat's $4k$ splitting: primes $\equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ vs $\equiv 3 \pmod{4}$. The coefficient halves to $\frac{n-1}{10}$.

3. The Geometric Seduction

At this point the conjecture writes itself. The exponent is $\frac{4}{5}$. The coefficient involves 5. In biology, Kleiber's law gives metabolic rate $B \propto M^{3/4}$. Here we have $c_n \propto r^{4/5}$. Where else do 4 and 5 appear together geometrically?

3.1 Hypersphere surface-area-to-volume ratios

The volume and surface area of the unit $d$-sphere are

$$V_d = \frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)}, \qquad S_d = \frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)} = d\,V_d.$$

For a sphere of radius $r$: $V_d(r) = V_d\,r^d$, $S_d(r) = S_d\,r^{d-1}$, so $\frac{S_d(r)}{V_d(r)} = \frac{d}{r}.$

$d$$V_d$ (unit)$S_d$ (unit)$S_d/V_d$

For $d=5$: $\frac{S_5(r)}{V_5(r)} = \frac{\frac{8}{3}\pi^2 r^4}{\frac{8}{15}\pi^2 r^5} = \frac{5}{r}.$ The exponents 4 and 5 together. The coefficient $\frac{1}{5}$. Everything matches.

Conjecture 3.1 (Original, 2021) The limiting growth function of the number density of primes between consecutive terms of polygon $n$-gon sequences is the Kleiber-like law given by the SA/V ratio of a 5-sphere: $\;c_n(r) \sim \frac{n-1}{5}\,r^{4/5}.$

It is a beautiful picture. The primes, those most discrete of objects, governed by the continuous geometry of higher-dimensional spheres.

It is also, as we shall now see, almost certainly wrong.

4. The Prime Number Theorem in Disguise

Four-fifths is not a new law. It is an old one wearing a mask. The argument uses only logarithmic differentiation and a single asymptotic approximation.

4.1 The PNT estimate for polygonal gaps

The PNT states $\pi(x) \sim \frac{x}{\ln x}$: the density of primes near $x$ is $\sim \frac{1}{\ln x}$. For an interval of width $\Delta$ near $x$, the expected prime count is $\sim \frac{\Delta}{\ln x}$.

For the $n$-gonal sequence at large $r$: $\Delta \approx (n-2)\,r$ and $x = P_n(r) \approx \frac{(n-2)\,r^2}{2}$. So:

(3) $$c_n^{\,\text{PNT}}(r) \approx \frac{(n-2)\,r}{\ln\!\left(\frac{(n-2)\,r^2}{2}\right)}.$$
Exercise 4.1 — Check the approximation For $n=4$ at $r=278$: $P_4(278) = 278^2 = 77{,}284$ and gap $= 2(278)+1 = 557$. The approximation gives $2 \times 278 = 556$ and $\frac{2 \times 278^2}{2} = 77{,}284$ (exact for squares since $P_4(r)=r^2$).

4.2 The effective exponent on a log-log plot

On a log-log plot of $c_n(r)$ vs $r$, the local slope is

$$\alpha_{\text{eff}}(r) = \frac{d[\ln c_n]}{d[\ln r]}.$$

If $c_n(r) = A\,r^\beta$, then $\alpha_{\text{eff}} = \beta$ exactly. A varying $\alpha_{\text{eff}}$ means the data is not a pure power law.

Key Result — Effective exponent from the PNT Let $L = \ln\!\bigl(\frac{(n-2)r^2}{2}\bigr)$. Then $\ln c \approx \ln(n-2) + \ln r - \ln L$. Differentiating with respect to $u = \ln r$: $$\frac{d(\ln c)}{du} = 1 - \frac{1}{L}\cdot\frac{dL}{du} = 1 - \frac{2}{L},$$ since $\frac{dL}{du} = 2$ (the only $r$-dependent term in $L$ is $2\ln r$). Therefore: $$\boxed{\;\alpha_{\text{eff}}(r) = 1 - \frac{2}{\ln\!\left(\frac{(n-2)\,r^2}{2}\right)}\;}$$

4.3 When does $\alpha_{\text{eff}} = \frac{4}{5}$?

Setting $\alpha_{\text{eff}} = \frac{4}{5}$:

$$1 - \frac{2}{L} = \frac{4}{5} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; L = 10 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \frac{(n-2)\,r^2}{2} = e^{10} \approx 22{,}026.$$

The logarithm is the slowest-growing unbounded function. Any power law that approximates a logarithmic function over a finite range will look exact within that range. The $\frac{4}{5}$ is not wrong. It is a snapshot of a moving target.

The Salutary Lesson A pattern that holds for the first five hundred terms tells you nothing certain about the five-hundred-and-first, let alone the five-billionth. Physical science proceeds by induction: gather data, fit a law, test it. Number theory proceeds by deduction: state a theorem, prove it, know it holds for every integer to infinity. The gap between these modes is not a technicality. It is the whole point.

Exercise 5.1 — Tetrahedral numbers The tetrahedral numbers are cumulative sums of triangulars: $T(r) = \frac{r(r+1)(r+2)}{6}.$ Show that the gap $T(r+1) - T(r) = \frac{(r+1)(r+2)}{2} \sim r^2/2.$ This is a cubic sequence with quadratic gaps.

5.2 Effective exponent for degree-$d$ sequences

For leading term $\frac{A}{d!}\,r^d$ with gap $\sim \frac{A}{(d-1)!}\,r^{d-1}$ and location $x \sim \frac{A}{d!}\,r^d$, the PNT count is:

(4) $$c^{(d)}(r) \approx \frac{\frac{A}{(d-1)!}\,r^{d-1}}{\ln\!\left(\frac{A}{d!}\,r^d\right)}.$$

Differentiating as before:

Key Result — General effective exponent (in $r$) $$\boxed{\;\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(d)}(r) = (d-1) - \frac{d}{\ln\!\left(\frac{A}{d!}\,r^d\right)}\;}$$ This approaches $d-1$ from below as $r \to \infty$.

5.3 Exponent in terms of sequence values

For cross-dimensional comparison, plot count vs $x = P_n(r)$ itself. Since $x \sim \frac{A}{d!}r^d$, the gap is $\Delta \sim C\,x^{(d-1)/d}$ and the PNT count is:

(5) $$c(x) \approx \frac{C\,x^{(d-1)/d}}{\ln x} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(d)}(x) = \frac{d-1}{d} - \frac{1}{\ln x}.$$

5.4 Reconciling with the original observation

Why $\frac{4}{5}$ and not $\frac{1}{2}$? Because the original plots used $r$ as the independent variable, where $\alpha \to 1$ (not $\frac{1}{2}$). At $\ln x \approx 10$, the effective exponent in $r$ is $1 - \frac{2}{10} = \frac{4}{5}$. This coincidence — that $1 - \frac{1}{5} = \frac{4}{5}$ where $\frac{1}{5}$ comes from $\frac{2}{\ln(22{,}026)}$ and $22{,}026$ is the size of the polygonal numbers mid-range — is the entire content of the Kleiber analogy.

Exercise 5.2 — The cubic case For tetrahedral numbers ($d=3$, $A=1$), $P(r) \approx r^3/6$ and gap $\approx r^2/2$. Show that: $$\alpha_{\text{eff}}(r) = 2 - \frac{3}{\ln(r^3/6)}.$$ At what value of $r$ does $\alpha_{\text{eff}} = \frac{5}{3}$? (Answer: $\ln(r^3/6) = 9$, so $r^3/6 \approx 8{,}103$, giving $r \approx 37$.) Over the range $r = 30$–$100$, you would measure an effective exponent near 1.7.

6. What Remains Open

The exponent is explained. The coefficient is not.

The empirical law has $c_n(r) \approx \frac{n-1}{5}\,r^{4/5}$. The PNT gives $c_n^{\,\text{PNT}}(r) \approx \frac{(n-2)\,r}{\ln\bigl(\frac{(n-2)r^2}{2}\bigr)}$. Over the range where $\alpha_{\text{eff}} \approx 0.8$, a power-law regression on the PNT curve yields a coefficient that depends on $n$. Whether it can be written as $\frac{n-1}{5}$ (note: $n-1$, not $n-2$) for a structural reason, or whether this is another finite-range artefact, is a genuine question.

Project suggestion Compute $c_n(r)$ for $n = 3, \ldots, 15$ and $r = 1, \ldots, 10{,}000$. For each $n$, fit $c_n(r) = A_n\,r^{\beta_n}$ over sliding windows of width 200. Plot $\beta_n$ as a function of window centre. Verify the drift. Then plot $A_n$ against $n$ for a fixed window. Is $A_n \propto n-1$ stable?

7. Epistemological Coda

Numbers are patient. They will confirm almost any pattern you propose, for a while.

The physical sciences proceed by induction. You gather data, fit a law, test it. If the law survives, you accept it provisionally. This method sent spacecraft to the moon and vaccines into arms.

But number theory is not physics. Its objects are infinite. A property of the first $10^{12}$ primes may not hold for the next. The Mertens conjecture, plausible up to enormous values, is false. The first counterexample to the Pólya conjecture occurs at $n = 906{,}150{,}257$. Skewes' number reminds us that $\pi(x) > \mathrm{Li}(x)$ must eventually happen, though no one has ever observed it.

The $\frac{4}{5}$ law is a gentler example. It is not false, rather it describes a real feature of the data at finite range. But it mistakes a shadow for a source. The source is the prime number theorem. The shadow shifts as you move the lamp.

The lesson: when confronted with a numerical pattern, always ask what the asymptotic theory predicts. Compute the effective exponent analytically. Check whether the pattern is a feature of the structure or a feature of the window. The tools of logarithmic differentiation, asymptotic expansion, the PNT itself are not difficult. They are the minimum equipment for navigating a landscape where the finite and the infinite coexist uneasily.
