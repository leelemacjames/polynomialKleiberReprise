Empiricism, Infinity, and the Limits of Pattern

A Kleiber-like Growth Law for Prime Density

Lee McCulloch-James · October 2021 · with commentary

1
The cast of characters

Polygonal Numbers

Consider the familiar figurate numbers. Three pebbles form a triangle. Four form a square. The ancient Greeks arranged stones into regular shapes and counted. Each shape generates a quadratic sequence. Triangular numbers grow as 1, 3, 6, 10, 15. Square numbers as 1, 4, 9, 16, 25. Pentagonal, hexagonal, and onward. All of them are unified by a single formula.

n-gonal number generator Pn(r) = ((n − 2)r² − (n − 4)r) / 2

Here n is the polygon degree (3 for triangles, 4 for squares, etc.) and r is the term index. The gap between consecutive terms is linear in r:

Gap between consecutive terms Pn(r+1) − Pn(r) = (n − 2)r + 1

So the gaps widen steadily. And as they widen, they swallow more and more prime numbers. The question is: how fast?

Sieving the gaps

Primes Between the Milestones

Primes thin out as numbers grow. The prime number theorem quantifies this: near a large number x, roughly one in every ln(x) integers is prime. But this is an average statement. What happens when you look at specific structured intervals — the gaps between consecutive terms of a polygonal sequence?

Each polygonal sequence lays down a set of milestones along the number line. Between consecutive milestones lies an interval of width (n−2)r+1. We can sieve each interval and simply count how many primes fall inside. The result is a sequence of counts — one for each value of r — that we can plot and fit.

3
The pattern emerges

A Four-Fifths Power

Across all polygon degrees tested (n = 3 through n = 15), the best-fit power law for the prime count between the r-th and (r+1)-th n-gonal numbers is:

Empirical growth law countn(r) ≈ ((n − 1) / 5) · r4/5

Two features stand out. The exponent 4/5 is invariant across polygon degree. The coefficient scales linearly as (n−1)/5. This holds even when you split the primes by Fermat's criterion — those congruent to 1 mod 4 versus 3 mod 4 — except the coefficient halves to (n−1)/10, as expected from partitioning into two roughly equal families.

The fit is not approximate. It is startlingly good. In the chart below, every polygon from 3-gon to your chosen maximum is overlaid. The dashed purple line is the empirical 4/5 law for the largest n shown. Look at the slope. Every sequence shares it.

A geometric echo

Hyperspheres and Kleiber's Law

Kleiber's law in biology states that an animal's metabolic rate scales as the 3/4 power of its body mass. The exponent arises (in one popular account) from the geometry of fractal transport networks. Here we have a 4/5 power with a factor of 1/5. Consider the unit hyperspheres.

Hypersphere Geometry by Dimension
dVolume Vd(1)Surface Area Sd(1)SA / V

The 5-dimensional case is highlighted. Unit sphere volume peaks at d = 5; surface area peaks at d = 7.

For the 5-sphere, SA/V = (8/3)π²r⁴ / ((8/15)π²r⁵) = 5/r. The ratio of the r-dependent parts gives r⁴/r⁵. The exponents 4 and 5 together. The coefficient 1/5. Everything matches.

The conjecture at this point was: the 5-sphere SA/V ratio forms the natural limiting envelope for prime density growth across all polygon sequences. For the cubic analogues (tetrahedral numbers, etc.), the corresponding exponent would be 5/6 — the SA/V ratio of a 6-dimensional hypersphere.

It is a beautiful picture. The primes, those most discrete and arithmetic of objects, governed in their local density by the continuous geometry of higher-dimensional spheres. It is also, as we shall now see, almost certainly wrong.

5
What infinity knows

The Prime Number Theorem in Disguise

Four-fifths is not a new law. It is an old one wearing a mask. The argument is short. For large r, the n-gonal number is approximately Pn(r) ≈ (n−2)r²/2, and the gap is (n−2)r. The PNT predicts the prime count in the gap is:

PNT prediction count(r) ≈ (n−2)r / ln((n−2)r²/2)

Now ask: on a log-log plot, what is the slope? Differentiate:

Effective log-log slope α(r) = d[ln(count)] / d[ln(r)] = 1 − 2 / ln((n−2)r²/2)

This is always less than 1 and approaches 1 from below as r → ∞. It equals exactly 4/5 when ln((n−2)r²/2) = 10, i.e. when (n−2)r²/2 ≈ 22,026. For squares (n = 4), that gives r ≈ 148. For triangles (n = 3), r ≈ 210. These are precisely the ranges where the fit was calibrated.

The chart below makes this visible. It plots α(r) for several polygon degrees. Every curve crosses through 0.8 in the r = 100–300 range. That is the window of the spreadsheet. That is where the 4/5 was measured.

Extend the range to r = 2,000 and the effective exponent is already 0.84. At r = 10,000 it would be 0.87. At r = 1,000,000 it would be 0.92. The exponent drifts inexorably toward 1. The 4/5 was never a fixed law. It was a snapshot.

The salutary lesson

What Finite Data Cannot Say About Infinite Structures

Numbers are patient. They will confirm almost any pattern you propose, for a while. The 4/5 power law fitted the data across every polygon degree tested, from n = 3 to n = 15, over hundreds of terms. It outperformed the PNT estimate at every point in the range. It had a clean coefficient. It invited a geometric explanation. All of this was true. None of it was proof.

The hypersphere connection — the surface-area-to-volume ratio of a 5-sphere yielding the same exponent and the same factor of 5 — is the kind of coincidence that mathematics produces in abundance. The number 4/5 appears wherever something approaches 1 from below and you happen to look at a range where 2/ln(x) ≈ 0.2. That is not geometry. That is the logarithm being slow.

This is the oldest tension in the subject. Physical science proceeds by induction: gather data, fit a law, test it against new data. Number theory proceeds by deduction: state a theorem, prove it, and know it holds for every integer from here to infinity. The gap between these two modes is not a technicality. It is the whole point. A pattern that holds for the first five hundred terms tells you nothing certain about the five-hundred-and-first, let alone the five-billionth.

The prime number theorem is proved. The 4/5 law is not wrong — it is incomplete. It describes a real feature of the data at finite range. But it mistakes a shadow for a source. The source is the PNT. The shadow shifts as you move the lamp.

What remains open. The coefficient (n−1)/5 is still unexplained. The PNT gives the exponent but does not obviously produce a clean linear dependence on polygon degree with denominator 5. Whether this coefficient has a deeper origin — or is itself another finite-range artefact — remains a genuine question.
A note on cubic sequences

The polyhedral analogues (tetrahedral numbers, etc.) generate cubic sequences with quadratic gaps. The same PNT analysis gives an effective exponent α(r) = 1 − 3/ln(P(r)), which at comparable ranges would be slightly higher than the quadratic case — consistent with the originally conjectured 5/6. The same caution applies: the exponent is not fixed, it drifts.
